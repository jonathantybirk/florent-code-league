# Similar bot-programming competitions

> **Measured before the Aug 4 turret patch (fcode ≤ 2.3.3).** Everything below was
> measured when turrets were stronger. The 2026-08-04 balance pass (fcode 2.3.4)
> changed the Gunner to 25 HP (was 40), 20 Ti (was 10), +20% cost scaling (was
> +10%), 7 damage (was 10) and 4 ammo per shot (was 2), and the Sentinel to 40 HP
> (was 30) on a 2-round reload (was 3). That balance pass is the only rules change
> in 2.3.4, so conclusions here that do not turn on turret strength still stand —
> but every turret-heavy number needs re-measuring before it is trusted again.

## Florent Code League

Florent describes its event as a three-week competition in which teams program an autonomous system to secure resources, coordinate units, and adapt to opponents. Its public page says matches are played on a live ladder and that the top 16 teams advance to a Stockholm final with a €20,000 prize pool. A Florent announcement described the event as a collaboration with Cambridge Battlecode.

Sources: [Florent Code League](https://league.florent.vc/), [Florent announcement](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/florent-vc_the-next-generation-of-nordic-founders-will-activity-7470449375492878336-eaB2)

The locally archived Florent rules describe cores, builders, harvesters, conveyors, titanium, turrets, and other buildings. Those descriptions are preserved in [the local rules overview](../../../docs/scraped-originals/game.code.florent.vc/docs/game-rules-overview.txt).

## Cambridge Battlecode

Cambridge Battlecode describes itself as a six-week programming competition inspired by MIT Battlecode. According to its site, teams write autonomous bots, test them on a ladder, and qualifying teams play in live finals.

Sources: [Cambridge Battlecode](https://battlecode.cam/), [2026 specification overview](https://docs.battlecode.cam/spec/overview)

## MIT Battlecode

MIT Battlecode describes its game as a contest in which teams write code for autonomous robot armies that manage resources and fight opponents. Its “About” page names pathfinding, resource management, communication, combat tactics, local decisions, global decisions, and limited computation as recurring problems in the event. The organizer publishes prior winners and postmortems on its past-years page.

Sources: [MIT Battlecode](https://battlecode.org/), [About Battlecode](https://battlecode.org/about.html), [Past years](https://battlecode.org/past.html)

## Robocode

Robocode describes itself as a programming game in which participants write autonomous virtual tank robots and do not control them directly during battle. Its official history states that Mat Nelson began the project in late 2000, IBM released it through alphaWorks in July 2001, and it became open source in 2005. The same history says Robot Battle was an inspiration.

Sources: [Robocode introduction](https://robocode.dev/articles/intro.html), [Robocode history](https://robocode.dev/articles/history.html)

## Lux AI Challenge

Lux describes its event as a 1v1 AI programming competition and multi-agent reinforcement-learning benchmark involving resource gathering and allocation. The organizer reports more than 22,000 submissions from more than 1,100 teams in 80 countries for Season 1. Its Season 2 rules placed factories and robots on a 48-by-48 grid, with robots collecting ice and ore, factories refining resources and creating robots, and lichen determining score. The organizer says competitors have submitted heuristic, brute-force-search, reinforcement-learning, and imitation-learning systems.

Sources: [Lux AI Challenge](https://www.lux-ai.org/), [Season 2 specification](https://www.lux-ai.org/specs-s2)

## Halite

Two Sigma described Halite II as an artificial-intelligence programming challenge. Its official result announcement says the second season ended on 29 January 2018 and was won by the competitor using the name `reCurs3`.

Source: [Two Sigma's Halite II result announcement](https://www.twosigma.com/articles/halite-ii-concludes-winners-announced/)

## Multi-Agent Programming Contest

The Multi-Agent Programming Contest states that it began in 2005 to identify multi-agent-system problems, collect benchmarks, and gather test cases requiring coordinated action. Its historical scenarios include gold miners, cow herding, agents on Mars, agents in a city, and Agents Assemble. In the 2022 scenario, agents with limited local vision coordinated on a grid to assemble and deliver block structures.

Sources: [MAPC overview and history](https://multiagentcontest.org/), [MAPC 2022](https://multiagentcontest.org/2022/)

## Entelect Challenge

Entelect describes its Challenge as a competition in which entrants program a bot to play a game designed by Entelect. Its 2024 game included claiming land, attacking players, and collecting power-ups, followed by qualifying tournaments and an eight-player live final. An organizer representative stated in the event report that a simple bot could be effective with the right strategy.

Source: [Entelect's 2024 finals report](https://culture.entelect.co.za/news/2024-entelect-challenge-finals/)

## BotBattle

BotBattle describes itself as a month-long online competition founded in 2023. Entrants submit programs that play a multiplayer arcade game; the service repeatedly matches bots during the development period and publishes a ranking before a closing ceremony.

Source: [BotBattle](https://www.botbattle.be/)

## Battlesnake

Battlesnake describes itself as a competitive programming game in which a live web server controls a snake through an API. Bots seek food, avoid opponents, and attempt to survive. The service runs automated rated leaderboards and supports tournaments. Its leaderboard documentation says bots are continuously ranked using TrueSkill after scheduled batches of matches.

Sources: [Battlesnake introduction](https://docs.battlesnake.com/), [leaderboards](https://docs.battlesnake.com/guides/leaderboards), [tournaments](https://docs.battlesnake.com/guides/tournaments)

## CodinGame bot programming

CodinGame hosts persistent bot-programming games and time-bounded challenges. Its catalogue labels games by subjects that include multi-agent control, resource management, pathfinding, state machines, simulation, minimax, and Monte Carlo tree search. One listed game, *A Code of Ice and Fire*, places opposing headquarters on a grid and asks programs to capture territory, build armies, and destroy the opposing headquarters.

Sources: [CodinGame bot catalogue](https://www.codingame.com/multiplayer/bot-programming), [A Code of Ice and Fire](https://www.codingame.com/multiplayer/bot-programming/a-code-of-ice-and-fire)

## Screeps: Arena

Screeps, LLC describes Screeps: Arena as asynchronous 1v1 PvP in which JavaScript controls autonomous units. Its product description names resource gathering, base building, unit creation, rating-based matchmaking, and arenas with different rules and goals.

Source: [Screeps: Arena product page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1137320/Screeps_Arena/)

## IEEE Conference on Games competitions

The IEEE Conference on Games 2026 competition page lists several game-agent events. It describes Planet Wars as an RTS in which agents compete for control of planets and destroy enemy units across varying game parameters, with a live leaderboard updated several times per day. The same page lists Battlesnake Blackout, Tales of Tribute, Codenames AI, and several other game-agent competitions.

Source: [IEEE CoG 2026 competitions](https://cog2026.org/competitions)

## CodeCup

CodeCup describes itself as an online programming competition in which submitted programs play a selected game against one another. Its site retains an archive of past contests and separates game rules, technical submission rules, and competition/ranking rules.

Sources: [CodeCup rules](https://www.codecup.nl/rules.php), [past contests](https://www.codecup.nl/archives.php)
